Preface

The real subject isn't the network. It's the model.

In a multi-brand organization, the question of synergy is often framed around pooling: purchasing, processes, systems, data or support functions. But pooling is not a strategy in itself.

A synergy creates value when it simplifies the model, speeds up decisions, and simultaneously improves the customer experience and operational performance.

Pool what creates strength.
Preserve what creates agility.
Govern what creates risk.
Chapter 1

Twelve milestones: from silos to the networked enterprise

The path to the hybrid model runs through twelve recognizable stages in most multi-brand organizations, whether company-owned, franchised or mixed:

  1. Siloed brands — strong autonomy, local practices.
  2. Buying groups — the first structuring pooling effort.
  3. BU coexistence — franchise, company-owned stores and different formats.
  4. Network friction — efficiency losses, diverging rules.
  5. Rival e-commerce — digital runs as an isolated BU.
  6. Convergence — the pursuit of network service equity.
  1. Cross-BU visibility — comparison and analytical steering.
  2. Over-centralization — the opposite risk: losing local agility.
  3. Augmented stock — cross-network dispatching, faster decisions.
  4. Risk Radar — alerts on stockouts, dependencies and margins.
  5. Group framework — processes, roles and back-office clarified.
  6. Hybrid model — progressive unification without erasing specifics.

Chapter 2

Target convergence architecture

One common backbone, several commercial engines. The goal isn't to make every channel identical — it's to make their business rules compatible: shared reference data, explicit accountability, shareable data and comparable indicators.

⚠ Watch point
The hybrid model fails when governance becomes blurred: who decides? who funds it? who owns the data? who's accountable for the outcome? who can deviate from the standard?
Chapter 5

Data becomes the contract between business units

Without shared reference data, synergy stays theoretical. Master data isn't a technical project — it's a governance mechanism: definition, owner, quality, update frequency, sharing rules and authorized use.

KPIs to track

  • Reference data quality
  • Data freshness
  • Completeness rate
  • Cross-BU discrepancies
  • Time needed to produce a Group indicator
Chapter 6

Risk Radar: seeing dependencies before the break

Synergy increases power — and sometimes the blast radius of an incident. In a multi-BU model, risk must be read at two levels: the risk specific to each entity, and the propagation risk created by dependence on the shared core.

From signal to decision: five possible responses

ResponseWhen to use itTypical example
ReduceThe dependency can be lowered without losing valueDiversify a single logistics supplier
ShareThe risk is better carried collectivelyPool a cross-BU safety stock
SecureThe dependency is strategic and must be hardenedDuplicate hosting of the Data/MDM core
AcceptThe cost of treatment exceeds the risk's valueA known, budgeted seasonal variation
MonitorThe risk is latent, not yet materializedGradual ramp-up of a new channel
Reading principle

The Risk Radar surfaces zones of concentration and triggers a decision — it does not replace the executive committee's judgment.

Chapter 7

From vision to execution

A four-step trajectory. Success isn't measured by the number of harmonized processes, but by reduced friction, faster decisions, and the network's ability to absorb change without losing agility.

2. ArbitrateDefine the common core and the zones of freedom.
3. SteerInstall KPIs, governance, accountability and the Risk Radar.
4. ScaleIndustrialize what works and fix what doesn't.
